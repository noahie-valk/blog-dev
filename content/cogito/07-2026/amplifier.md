![frank ocean blues](https://noahie.xyz/images/frank-ocean-blues-2.png)

When certain albums come to mind, I am instantly transported back to the summer before starting my freshman year of college. Frank Ocean had just dropped [Blonde](https://blonded.ffm.to/blonde); not only did I feel myself getting immersed in this seminal moment in music, but it also got me through a rough transition in life. I've [written about it](https://noahie.xyz/blog/cogito/03-2026/pilgrim) already, so I won't go over those details again; however, I think it's important to understand what this album did—not just for me, but for so many people my age at that time. I remember *so distinctly* putting this album on and hearing the first few seconds of [Nikes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=diIFhc_Kzng), and I just knew it was going to stay with me for the rest of my life. Almost ten years later, and that's still true. For all the heartbreak and hard times I've been through since then, this album has been there when I feel those intense moments of longing and yearning—and the tears inevitably begin to flow.

Frank Ocean, born Christopher Edwin Breaux, was raised in New Orleans. He began his career as a ghostwriter for other artists, but came into his own when he joined the hip-hop collective [Odd Future](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_Future) back in 2010. It was clear even in those days that Frank was a standout act, and after releasing a few mixtapes and a studio album named [Channel Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_Orange), his Grammy wins confirmed he was destined for musical greatness. Unfortunately, there was some dispute with his record label, and his next few releases were shrouded in rebellious mystery. I had been following his work closely since Channel Orange, and while I didn't catch the press at the time, I was waiting anxiously for the new release; when it finally came, it was so explosively good that I'll be not-so-bold as to say it's the greatest album of the 2010s—and it's not even close.

Growing up, I was enamored with jazz as a saxophone player, and seeing contemporary acts like Frank Ocean take those beautiful sounds from soul, funk, and R&B—and recreate them for a young listener's ears—was one of the most beautiful privileges I've experienced as a deep appreciator of art and music. It's so easy for older people to say that music isn't like what it used to be or that it's dying; they simply *can't process* the consistent levels of quality that keep coming out every year. Sure, it changes as new generations try to reinvent themselves, but at the heart of it is the same lyrical melancholy and opulence we've seen for the last several decades; even with the aesthetic changes, the soul of the music is still undeniable, flaws and all.

The only trade I really know and understand is the arts. Everything else feels so hollow and performative; even with the irony that much of art is performance, it still feels like the only authentic expression there is. Listening to Frank made me realize that there's a deep price to being authentic; it scares most people, and even if you do everything you can to be accepted, there will still be a social price to pay. I know I have to deal with that every day. I put myself in the furnace every day I wake up, and I can't bear it most of the time. To do the right thing, maintain love, and never be angry is a tall order, and I still find myself failing most of the time. When I see everyone else so concerned with trivial things like status and security, it makes me remember to heed the chorus from *Solo*:

*It's Hell on Earth, and the city's on fire*

*Inhale, in Hell, there's Heaven*

*There's a bull and a matador dueling in the sky*

*Inhale, in Hell, there's Heaven*

I remember when Frank's [Open letter on Tumblr](https://genius.com/Frank-ocean-open-letter-on-tumblr-annotated) was circulating, and it was so endearing to see someone bring something so human to a space so culturally profane and ostracized, especially in 2012. It showed that homosexuality isn't this sacrilegious thing, but can come from that same undying and unyielding source of love we all aspire toward in our own imperfect and material ways. Even if I don't condone homosexual behavior due to my religious reasons, I will always empathize with the pained cries of love, unrequited or otherwise. I can't claim to know why God makes us love the ways we do, but I know that even in the wailing and thrashing we all do behind closed doors, there's something greater whispering to all of us, and all we have to do is listen.

Thankfully, Frank's got an amplifier for us.
